Yue (Troy) Cao is a Research Fellow in the Department of Mechanical Engineering at the University of Michigan. He holds a Ph.D. in Electrical Engineering from the University of Kentucky. His research focuses on the integration of control and information theory to design optimal and robust intelligent systems for applications in manufacturing, robotics, and AI inference. Currently, his work centers on robotic Wire and Arc Additive Manufacturing (WAAM) and intelligent stamping processes.

Physics-Structured Molten Pool Modeling Across Arc Processes

Physics-Informed ModelingAdditive Manufacturing

Physics-Structured Molten Pool Modeling Across Arc Welding and Additive Manufacturing

Apr 2026 – Present · University of Michigan

The molten pool is the central phenomenon of arc welding and arc-based additive manufacturing, and its evolution decides the quality of the deposit. Yet pool models are built one process at a time and must be redesigned whenever the joint, feedstock, sensors, or welding method change, although the same conservation laws govern every arc process.

This project builds a single physics-structured, dissipative neural state-space framework for these processes. A pretrained vision foundation model, regularized by a neural ODE prior, first extracts a compact pool state from the video of one experiment without labels. That state then enters a lumped energy balance that closes into a two-state model: a fast pool state that relaxes toward an input-dependent equilibrium, and a slow state for the heat accumulated in the workpiece, which is never measured and is learned only through long-horizon prediction.

Tested on GTAW, GMAW, and WAAM, the same two-state structure appears in every case, with only the sensing front end adapted to each process.

Autonomous Robotic Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing: Sensing, Digital Twin, and Closed-Loop Control

Apr 2026 – Present · University of Michigan · In progress

This project builds a robotic WAAM system in which process and part states are sensed, predicted, corrected, and executed in a closed loop. The work spans two scales: single beads and walls, where local links between process state, action, and geometry are learned, and complex parts such as turbine components, where those local models guide decisions on global geometry, heat, and robot motion.

Planned platform: an HDR camera for arc, wire, and melt-pool morphology; a thermal camera and pyrometer for the thermal state; a 3D camera for layer geometry; and a KUKA robot with a positioner for deposition.

Proposed directions
  1. Multisource monitoring and state estimation. Fuse camera, thermal, and 3D data into one estimate of the melt pool, bead, and part, with uncertainty.
  2. Sensor-corrected digital twin. Physics models and fast learned surrogates of thermal history, bead growth, and distortion, updated continuously by measurements.
  3. Hierarchical process and geometry control. An inner loop regulates the melt pool and bead along a path; an outer loop corrects accumulated shape error after each layer.
  4. Process-aware robotic deposition planning. Robot and positioner motion that preserves process conditions while respecting reachability, singularity, and collision limits.

Physics-Informed Reconstruction of Molten Pool Flow

Physics-Informed ModelingAdditive Manufacturing

Physics-Informed Reconstruction of Molten Pool Flow from High-Speed Video

2025 – 2026 · University of Kentucky

Flow inside the molten pool governs heat transfer and solidification, but it is hidden beneath a bright, moving surface. From high-speed video of a GTAW-based additive process, optical flow gives sparse surface velocities, and a physics-informed neural network that embeds the Navier–Stokes equations reconstructs the complete velocity and pressure fields while identifying the arc force and effective viscosity.

Three wire positions produce two distinct convection patterns, and a time-conditioned network captures the flow driven by droplet impact. Across the cases, the identified arc force is consistent and, with the pressure field, explains the observed flow patterns; low Reynolds numbers confirm laminar flow.

Humanoid Robot Welding with Multirate Whole-Body Control

Control & Robotics

Humanoid Robot Welding with Multirate Whole-Body Control

2025 – 2026 · University of Kentucky

Humanoid robots can walk to large, irregular workpieces in shipbuilding, field repair, and infrastructure, but a compliant whole-body kinematic chain makes sub-millimeter torch placement difficult.

In this framework, whole-body model predictive control plans the 19-joint configuration under balance constraints, and a faster inner loop corrects lateral and normal torch errors measured by seam-tracking and arc-voltage sensing. The two loops exchange information in both directions: the planner sends action limits based on the balance margin, and the inner loop returns offset estimates that shift the planned trajectory.

In a simulated weld pass, the closed loop reduced the critical lateral and normal torch errors from millimeters to about a tenth of a millimeter, a precision that whole-body control alone did not reach.

Latent Dynamics and Imitation-Learning-Augmented Optimal Control

Control & RoboticsPhysics-Informed Modeling

Latent Dynamics Modeling and Imitation-Learning-Augmented Optimal Control

2025 – 2026 · University of Kentucky

Autonomous control needs a model of how the process evolves. Neural ordinary differential equations learn continuous-time weld pool dynamics in a learned latent space. Of three architectures compared, a dissipative one with a Lyapunov constraint stays the most robust when the model adapts online, because the constraint keeps adapted models physically meaningful.

For control, a policy cloned from welder demonstrations serves as a prior inside an iterative linear quadratic regulator, which then optimizes only a correction around it. In simulation with model mismatch and disturbances, the combined controller converged in fewer iterations and rejected disturbances faster and with lower error than behavior cloning or iLQR alone.

Human Predictive Control with Generative AI

Human–Robot CollaborationGenerative AI & Monitoring

Human Predictive Control through Generative-AI-Enhanced Human–Robot Collaboration

2024 – 2026 · University of Kentucky

Skilled welders anticipate how the weld pool will change and act early, but remote operators who see only the live view tend to react late, because the weld pool responds slowly to their commands.

In a virtual-reality teleoperation system for gas tungsten arc welding, operators control the torch through motion capture while a generative model shows the weld pool predicted five seconds ahead, next to the live view and a reference pool. In experiments with three operators, the predicted view roughly halved the time needed to correct a speed disturbance and produced a more uniform backside bead, shifting control from reactive correction to proactive adjustment.

Generative AI & Monitoring

Generative Process Modeling and Bayesian Monitoring of Arc Welding

2024 – 2026 · University of Kentucky

Weld pool images carry rich process information but are expensive to label. These generative models learn a compact pool state without heavy annotation and predict how it evolves.

A VAE-GAN synthesizes realistic future pool images to support human operators; a hybrid-latent variational autoencoder learns structured latents that track the physical pool dimensions; and a dynamic variational autoencoder combined with a particle filter estimates hidden weld states online and transfers from GTAW to GMAW. The monitoring framework was developed with Prof. Hai Lin (University of Notre Dame).

WAAM Anomaly Detection from Noisy Industrial Labels

Generative AI & MonitoringAdditive Manufacturing

Reliable WAAM Anomaly Detection from Noisy Industrial Labels

2025 · University of Kentucky

In industrial additive manufacturing, quality labels are assigned to whole segments after inspection, while transient anomalies occupy only part of each segment, so many normal frames are labeled abnormal.

An iterative label refinement method, built on unsupervised feature learning with a variational autoencoder and Gaussian mixture model, corrects these labels during training and, on real industrial WAAM data, learns a reliable detector of spatter, gas disturbance, and arc instability. This work was carried out with Dr. Giulio Mattera (University of Naples Federico II) and Prof. Peng Wang (Case Western Reserve University).

Robotizing GTAW by Learning Human Response

Human–Robot CollaborationControl & Robotics

Robotizing GTAW by Learning Human Response

2024 – 2025 · University of Kentucky

In gas tungsten arc welding, skilled operators adjust travel speed from the look of the weld pool to reach the desired penetration. Copying the mapping from images to actions, however, learns only the surface of that skill.

From demonstrations collected in a VR teleoperation system, Shapley-based attribution identifies the visual features that drive an operator's speed adjustments, and an LSTM model learns the operator's response from those features. Deployed for autonomous welding, the learned model tracked the target backside weld width in closed loop and recovered after an added disturbance.

Human–Robot CollaborationControl & Robotics

Robotizing Double-Electrode GMAW through Human–Robot Collaboration

2023 – 2025 · University of Kentucky

Double-electrode GMAW adds a bypass electrode that separates heat input from metal deposition, which suits low-distortion additive manufacturing, but the bypass electrode must be positioned precisely in real time.

A VR-based dual-robot collaboration system lets a welder remotely manipulate the heavy torch with millimeter precision, producing stable welds with reduced heat input. Object detection and arc-voltage modeling explain how electrode position shapes the process, and the welders' demonstrations are used to teach robots to weld autonomously. This work received the 2025 IIW Henry Granjon Prize.

Adaptive Control of Weld Penetration in Pulsed GMAW

Control & Robotics

Adaptive Control of Weld Penetration in Pulsed GMAW

2018 – 2021 · Tianjin University

Weld penetration determines joint strength but forms out of sight beneath the pool, and camera-based sensing is often impractical on the factory floor.

Using only the welding machine's electrical signals, this work identified arc-voltage features that reveal penetration, fused them with a Kalman filter, and built a reduced-order nonlinear model of the process. An adaptive model predictive controller built on this model kept the backside weld width on target under sudden changes in heat dissipation, where open-loop welding lost consistency.
